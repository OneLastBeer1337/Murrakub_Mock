Part 1 · The problem

Two decisions, made together, before anything runs

A batch of AI workflows arrives. Each task could be served by several model profiles — (model, hardware tier, batch config) — differing in throughput, price, reliability and latency.

DecisionSymbolMeaning
Routingx[t][m]which profile serves task t — exactly one
Provisioningn[m]how many instances of m we pay for

Minimise the total bill. Do not exceed a GPU budget.

The circle

Routing determines load.
Load determines instance counts.
Instances consume the GPU budget.
A binding budget constrains routing.

Neither decision can be made first.

Where the difficulty actually lives — aggregate coupling. Capacity is bought in whole instances, not per task. So a task's marginal cost depends on whether its profile already has headroom — which depends on decisions not yet made. Two tasks that are each expensive to move can be cheap to move together.

Speaker notes

Do not rush this slide. If the room takes away one idea, it should be aggregate coupling. The next slide proves it with three tasks.

Common misread to head off: people assume this is bin-packing or scheduling. It is neither — there is no makespan and no time axis. It is facility location.

Part 2 · What you are ratifying · 1 of 6

The vocabulary — sets, parameters, variables

Sets

T           tasks, across all workflows in the batch
M           model profiles — an instantiable
            (model, hardware tier, config) triple
C(t) ⊆ M    profiles eligible for task t,
            after floor filtering

A profile is the unit that gets provisioned. A candidate is a profile eligible for a task. Same objects, viewed from different sides — worth saying once so nobody thinks they are two registries.

Decision variables

x[t][m] ∈ {0,1}   task t → profile m ∈ C(t)   level 1
n[m]    ∈ Z⁺      instances provisioned       level 2

"Two-level" refers to these two variable families, not to a hierarchy of solvers.

Parameters

load(t)    throughput demand of task t
thr(m)     throughput of ONE instance of m
gpu(m)     GPUs consumed by one instance
price(m)   cost of one instance over the horizon
B          total GPU budget
rel(m)     reliability of profile m        [0,1]
lat(t,m)   latency of task t on profile m
R_min(t)   reliability floor for task t
L_max(t)   latency ceiling for task t

R_min(t) is not an arbitrary input. It is the reliability the baseline would deliver for t — advisor guidance, 3 Sep. The goal is not to maximise reliability; it is to stay as good as not using this system.

Set it arbitrarily low and the optimiser will legally trade reliability for cost: given two profiles both clearing a 0.90 floor, it correctly takes 0.910 over 0.999 and saves 200. Anchoring the floor to the baseline enforces the requirement with no change to the objective or to (C1)–(C3).

Speaker notes

The R_min anchoring is the subtle one and it is worth the time. It is the difference between a floor that means something and a floor that can be satisfied by a system quietly getting worse. It also answers "why isn't reliability in the objective?" before that question is asked.

Note the elegance to claim here: the requirement is enforced entirely by how the floor is set, not by adding machinery. No new term, no new constraint.

Part 2 · What you are ratifying · 2 of 6

The objective — and the term we closed

minimize    Σ_{m ∈ M}  n[m] · price(m)

You pay for instances you provision, not for calls you make. That is the whole objective.

The term that was proposed — and closed

          + Σ_t Σ_m  x[t][m] · varcost(t, m)

A variable per-invocation term, for the case where executor pricing is usage-based rather than provisioning-based.

CLOSED, 2 September 2026: no. Not implemented, and ProfileSpec carries no varcost field.

Why it matters that this stays closed

  • Reopening it changes the objective signature everywhere — every track, every bound, every recorded number
  • It does not change the problem class, so it buys no new literature
  • It would break the clean statement that cost is a function of n alone — which is what makes the two-level structure legible

If you object to this, say so now. This is one of the two decisions on the sheet that is genuinely expensive to reverse later. The other is (C2).

Speaker notes

O1 is closed but it is a default that was adopted, not a law. If the team knows the real executor will bill per token, this is the moment to say it — after T0 it costs a rewrite of every result.

Part 2 · What you are ratifying · 3 of 6

The three constraints — and the two that are not constraints

(C1)  Σ_{m ∈ C(t)} x[t][m] = 1                              ∀ t ∈ T
      every task is assigned exactly one profile

(C2)  Σ_{t : m ∈ C(t)} x[t][m] · load(t)  ≤  n[m] · thr(m)   ∀ m ∈ M
      provisioned throughput covers routed load

(C3)  Σ_{m ∈ M} n[m] · gpu(m)  ≤  B
      total GPUs within budget

Floors are applied by construction

C(t) = { m ∈ M : rel(m) ≥ R_min(t)
                 and lat(t,m) ≤ L_max(t) }

Three reasons this is not a constraint in the program:

  • It keeps floors out of the program entirely
  • It preserves the LP relaxation's validity — a filtered pool is still a clean 0/1 assignment problem
  • It matches P3, feasibility first, cost second — a floor is never weighed against money

No linking constraint is needed

A reader expecting facility location will look for x[t][m] ≤ y[m] and not find it. It is redundant here:

if x[t][m] = 1 and load(t) > 0
   then (C2) already forces n[m] ≥ 1

The capacity constraint does the opening constraint's job, because capacity is bought in modular units rather than switched on. Adding the link would be harmless and useless.

This is a good question to be asked. It shows the formulation was reasoned about rather than copied.

Speaker notes

If anyone is going to object to §1, it will be here. Ask explicitly, constraint by constraint.

The no-linking-constraint point is worth volunteering — an examiner who knows facility location will notice its absence, and it is much better to have explained it than to be asked.

Part 2 · What you are ratifying · 4 of 6

Where the coupling lives — and why (C2) is the whole difficulty

ConstraintCouplesConsequence
(C1)Profiles, through each task A task's choice is exclusive — taking one profile forecloses the others
(C2) Tasks to instances, and tasks to each other across workflows sharing a profile This is the multi-workflow interaction. Two tasks in different workflows contend only if they route to the same profile
(C3)All profiles, through one budgetGlobal scarcity

The integrality gap lives in (C2)

n[m] must cover a ceiling of aggregate load over throughput. The LP relaxation returns fractional instance counts; rounding up costs real GPUs.

This is why the LP bound is not tight, and why an integer-aware method can beat it. It is the entire justification for Track B existing.

Aggregate coupling lives in (C2) too

A task's marginal cost depends on whether its chosen profile already has headroom. Routing task 1 to profile X may cost a whole instance — or nothing — depending on assignments not yet made.

Track A's central difficulty, and the thing the three-task fixture demonstrates in four lines.

This slide answers the exit criterion. "Which constraint couples workflows?" — (C2). Everything hard about this problem, and both reasons the heuristics are interesting, are in one constraint.

Speaker notes

If the room only remembers one slide from Part 2, make it this one. Two different difficulties — the integrality gap and aggregate coupling — both live in (C2), and they motivate two different tracks. That is not a coincidence worth glossing over.

Tie it back: the fixture on the earlier slide is aggregate coupling, and the reason the LP bound is loose is the integrality gap. One constraint, both stories.

Problem class — and why naming it correctly earned us three things

This is a modular capacitated facility location problem with a budget constraint.

Facility location termHere
Facility siteModel profile m
Opening multiple modular units at a siten[m] instances
CustomerTask t
Assignmentx[t][m]
Facility capacityn[m] · thr(m)
Global side constraintGPU budget (C3)

"Modular" is the word doing the work — capacity is opened in whole units, not switched on once.

Why the identification matters

1It justifies Lagrangian relaxation properly. Relaxing the assignment constraints is the classical method for capacitated facility location. Track B is not an arbitrary pick — it is the textbook method for this class
2It predicted the decomposition. Relaxing (C1) yields one subproblem per profile. That is the standard result — and it contradicted v1's claim of per-workflow decomposition. The literature caught our error before any code did
3It gives us a literature to cite beyond the two LLM-serving papers, which is what makes Chapter 2 defensible

Point 2 is the one to tell. Naming the problem class correctly found a design error for free — v1 predicted the wrong decomposition, and the textbook said so before we wrote a line.

Speaker notes

This is where "our problem is textbook" turns from a concession into an asset. Knowing the class told us which relaxation to build, predicted its structure, and corrected a wrong claim in v1.

Expect: "so what is novel?" — the answer is the loop, and it is the next slide.

Part 2 · What you are ratifying · 6 of 6

What is deliberately not modelled

Recorded so the divergence from Murakkab is explicit rather than accidental. Every line here is a decision, not an omission.

Not modelledConsequence, and why
No time dimension Steady-state provisioning over one horizon. No arrival dynamics, no queueing, no autoscaling. This is why there is no makespan term — and why HEFT was dropped
No precedence DAG edges determine data flow at execution time but do not enter the optimisation — exactly as in both source papers
Parallelism config is not a decision TP/PP degree is baked into a profile rather than chosen. Murakkab and Cheng & Nguyen both decide it; we do not. This is our largest deliberate divergence
No accuracy maximisation Reliability is a floor only — and O10 confirmed that on 3 Sep
No multi-tenancy pricing or SLO tiersSingle-tenant, one horizon

The one to volunteer: parallelism configuration. Both source papers treat TP/PP degree as a decision variable and we do not — we push it into the profile definition. That makes our problem smaller than theirs, and an examiner who knows those papers will spot it. Say it first, and say why: profiles are measured artefacts in our design, so their internal configuration is an input, not a choice.

Speaker notes

This slide is insurance. Each line is somewhere an examiner could say "you have not handled X". Having a written, reasoned list turns each of those from a gap into a scope decision.

The precedence line is the most common misunderstanding — people assume a DAG implies scheduling. It does not, here, and both source papers agree.

Part 2 · Positioning

What is borrowed, and what is ours

Borrowed — and we say so first

The allocation problem is known: modular capacitated facility location with a budget constraint. Profiles are facilities, n[m] is units opened, tasks are customers, (C2) is facility capacity.

We claim no novelty here and present it as adopted. §1.8.

Ours — the closed loop

Measure profiles from execution, detect drift, re-optimise. Neither Murakkab nor Cheng & Nguyen does this — both take profiles as static inputs.

Advisor confirmed 3 Sep (O12): sufficient as the M1 contribution. Novelty is claimed in the loop, not the optimisation.

Part 3 · System design · 4 of 6

J3 in six steps — and the one that changed

What one allocation run does

1Take an immutable profile snapshot
2Initialise Provisioning State — zero instances, zero load, full budget
3Select strategy (A, B or C)
4Execute the track
5Verify: every task assigned; (C2) holds for every profile; (C3) holds
6Emit AllocationResult containing both x and n

Step 1 matters more than it looks: the snapshot is immutable, so a profile update landing mid-run cannot change the problem underneath the solver.

Step 5 is the invariant check — I1–I5, and it runs on every result from every track before it is allowed to leave.

The change: J9 no longer scopes

J9 previously read "re-optimise for affected workflows only", with a note doubting whether that is even well-defined under (C2).

It is well-defined. It is also vacuous.

  • Drift is detected on a profile
  • So the affected workflows are those routed to it
  • But a shared profile is used by 84–100% of workflows, even at twelve

Scoping correctly does the same work as a global run. Scoping narrower costs a mean 22% and up to 51%.

Resolved — O9. J9 re-invokes J3 over the whole batch. No scoping. This is one of the project's most useful negative results: it deleted a planned component rather than adding one.

Speaker notes

Tell this as a design win, not a retreat. We planned scoped re-optimisation, measured whether it was meaningful, found it was not, and removed it. The cheapest component is the one you do not build.

Decision 5 on the sign-off sheet is exactly this. The default is "drop scoping".

Part 3 · System design · 5 of 6

Provisioning State — the correction at the heart of v2

v1 modelled a slot pool, decremented per task. That was wrong. Under (C2)–(C3) a task consumes no resource directly — it adds load, which may or may not force a new instance. Getting this backwards makes every cost number wrong, and it is the single largest correction between v1 and v2.

Interface

ProvisioningState:
    instances(m)      -> int     # n[m]
    load(m)           -> float   # routed load on m
    gpusUsed()        -> int     # Σ n[m]·gpu(m)
    headroom(m)       -> float   # n[m]·thr(m) − load(m)

    costToAdmit(t, m) -> AdmitCost | Infeasible
    admit(t, m)       -> void
    release(t, m)     -> void
    snapshot()        -> StateSnapshot
    restore(snap)     -> void
AdmitCost {
    extraInstances  # ceil(shortfall / thr(m)),
                    # ZERO if headroom suffices
    extraGpus       # extraInstances · gpu(m)
    extraCost       # extraInstances · price(m)
}

costToAdmit is the most important operation in the system

It answers "what does routing this task here cost right now" — and its answer changes as other tasks are admitted.

That state-dependence is the aggregate-coupling problem, made explicit in an interface rather than hidden inside three separate algorithms.

Two details that carry weight

  • costToAdmit returns Infeasible when extraGpus would exceed remaining budget — so (C3) is enforced at the point of decision, not checked afterwards
  • snapshot/restore exist for Track A's multi-start and Track C's rounding repair — both need to try an assignment and take it back

One component, one bug surface. Because all three tracks go through this object, aggregate coupling is implemented once. If it is right, it is right everywhere.

Speaker notes

This is the design slide to be proud of. The hard part of the problem was isolated into one method with a name, rather than being spread across three algorithms where each would have got it subtly differently.

The extraInstances = 0 case is the fixture in miniature — it is exactly what greedy fails to look ahead to.

Part 3 · System design · 6 of 6

One decision rule, three strategies — the seam that makes P4 real

function selectProfile(task, pool, state, costAdjust):
    best      = None
    bestValue = +inf

    for m in pool:          # pool is C(t),
                            # already floor-filtered
        admit = state.costToAdmit(task, m)
        if admit is Infeasible:   # would exceed budget
            continue

        value = costAdjust(m, admit)
        if value < bestValue:
            best      = m
            bestValue = value

    if best is None:
        return Infeasible(
            reason="no profile admissible within budget",
            blockingTask=task.id, constraint="C3")
    return best

Complexity: O(|C(t)|) per task.

costAdjust is the entire difference between the tracks

TrackcostAdjust(m, admit)
A greedyadmit.extraCost
marginal provisioning cost only
B Lagrangianadmit.extraCost + λ[task]
plus the relaxed assignment multiplier
C repairadmit.extraCost

Three coordination strategies that look completely different at the top — construct greedily, dualise and iterate, relax and round — meet at one function and differ by one term.

Why this is worth showing at T0. It is the concrete answer to "are your tracks really comparable?" They share the eligibility filter, the state object, the feasibility test and the selection loop. What differs is exactly one expression — so a difference in results is a difference in coordination strategy, not in plumbing.

Speaker notes

This slide pre-empts the strongest methodological objection to any track comparison: that the tracks differ in a hundred small ways and the comparison is meaningless. Here it is one line.

Note the Infeasible return carries the blocking task and the violated constraint. That is P9 in practice — a failure is a structured object, not an exception.

Part 5 · T3 — and a correction to ourselves

The budget was never inert. Our instances were homogeneous.

What went wrong, and how we caught it

Both original generators set price = gpus × constant. That is a homogeneous fleet, where minimising cost and minimising GPUs are nearly the same objective — so the budget had almost nothing left to decide.

We only noticed because it contradicted our own premise. This project is about routing across heterogeneous profiles. A fleet where every GPU costs the same is the one case where that premise does not apply.

The check

Murakkab reports GPU count, energy and dollar cost as three separate numbers on one matched workload — and they move by three different factors (÷2.82, ÷3.72, ÷4.33). Cost per GPU falls to 0.65×. Price is not a multiple of GPU count.

So we built a third generator modelling a local, owned, heterogeneous fleet, changed nothing else, and re-ran. The result reversed.

Closes O7. The budget binds wherever price per GPU is not constant — which, on our own premise, is the normal case. F26's "nearly inert" describes our old instances, and should only ever be quoted that way.

Speaker notes

This is the strongest slide in the deck. It is the clearest evidence that our methodology is real rather than decorative: we suspected our own instrument, built a new one, and it overturned one of our own headline findings.

If asked "how do you know your generators are realistic?" — this is the answer. We do not, and that is exactly why there are three of them with deliberately opposing structure.

Note what is not claimed: the first two generators are not retracted. They are valid models of a rented homogeneous fleet.

Part 5 · T1 — and a confound we caught in ourselves

Track B's bound holds. What fails is finding a primal.

The mistake we made. Pooled, heterogeneous looked like −2.17 pp and T1 looked like it had collapsed. That was two populations with different mechanisms averaged together — the average of a real effect and an artefact.

Why the negative group is our fault, not the method's. Track B's step rule aims the multipliers at the gap between the dual value and a known feasible cost. With no incumbent there is no target. By Geoffrion the dual optimum is ≥ the LP bound, so a shortfall proves our multipliers are unconverged — it cannot mean the relaxation is weak.

Speaker notes

Volunteer that F34 was wrong. Being the people who caught it within hours is the point. The lesson generalises and is worth stating: before reporting a pooled difference, ask what would make an instance behave differently, and split on it.

If challenged "your bound is sometimes worse than the LP, isn't that disqualifying?" — theory forbids it at the dual optimum, so it localises the fault precisely. That is a better answer than a number.

The real open problem moved: it is Track B's feasibility (7 of 17), not its bound.

Part 5 · The differentiator

What the loop is actually for

A static allocator does not fail loudly under drift. It keeps reporting success while silently violating the reliability floor it was given.

Required floor
0.95
the constraint both systems were given
Static allocator delivers
0.542
while reporting no change
Adaptive loop delivers
0.938
detects drift, re-allocates

The statistic that survives audit

Paired difference +0.424, 95% CI [0.405, 0.442], n = 20, mean = median — no skew, a tight interval, and the effect holds strongly.

Point figures 0.542 / 0.938 are F24 (8 seeds, both systems starting correct, identical drift schedule). The paired interval is F30's audit at n = 20. Quote them as two separate things — they are.

The objection you will get: "your adaptive system costs 2.5× more."

It does. And it is not like-for-like — the static system is cheaper because it is failing its requirement. Comparing the cost of a system that meets its floor against one that does not is not a cost comparison.

Speaker notes

This is the number the room should remember. Not the speedup — that one was retracted.

The word doing the work is silently. A system that fails loudly is an operations problem; one that fails while reporting success is a correctness problem, and that is what justifies the whole loop.

Caveat to volunteer: this is measured against a simulated executor. Real execution is Semester 2 and is the biggest threat to this result.

Part 6 · Method

Four headline numbers were retracted — by us

All four failed the same way: a ratio of two means, which is not a typical ratio when either distribution has a tail.

Do not saySay instead
"~110× faster than the exact solver" Median speedup is 5×. Argue bounded latency: 0.106 ± 0.020 s against 12.3 ± 10.3 s. 12.283 / 0.106 = 116 — that was the quoted number
"the bound is 3–6× tighter than the LP" Paired difference 12.6 pp [9.5, 15.6]; median ratio ~2–2.5×
"consolidation halves Track C's gap" Median improvement is 0.00%. It fixes a rare, severe failure — real, but tail-carried
"subset consolidation is a twenty-fold improvement" Never worse on 72 paired instances. Median per-instance ratio 1.53×. And this ratio was unstable — 20.6× on one instance set, 2.41× on another

The rules that came out of it

  • Never divide two means. Report the paired per-instance difference and its interval
  • A paired interval that crosses zero means the effect is not established
  • Treat a bare N× claim with no interval as unverified
  • The audit is not a one-time pass — anything merged from a branch is unaudited whatever its finding number
  • Before reporting a pooled difference, ask what would make an instance behave differently, and split on it

Why this belongs in the presentation rather than the appendix.

Most projects present results. Very few can present the audit that caught their own. The fourth retraction was found a week after the first three, in material merged from a branch — which is how we learned that rule five above was needed at all.

Volunteer the limitations before you are asked. An examiner who has to extract a weakness trusts you less than one you hand it to.

Speaker notes

Deliver this as a section, not an apology. The framing is: we built the check that found these, and we ran it on ourselves twice more after the first pass.

If someone asks how many numbers are still unaudited — the honest answer is that the corrections table in poc_findings_summary.md is the live list, and every number in this deck is on the audited side of it.

Part 6 · Threats to validity

What none of this establishes

1 · Nothing about real workloads

Three synthetic generators. One author wrote the distributions, the tracks and the metrics. This is the strongest objection available against every number in this deck.

Our answer: three generators with deliberately opposing structure — and the third one overturned one of our own headline findings. That is what makes the check credible rather than decorative. It is still not real measurement.

2 · Nothing about real execution

The loop's execution stage is simulated. Every drift result — including the differentiator — rests on that simulator.

3 · Track B's feasibility, not its bound

7 of 17 on heterogeneous instances. The bound is fine wherever it finds a primal; finding one is the problem. This is now the interesting open problem.

4 · Runtime claims rest on our implementation

"Track B is ~100× slower" describes a pure-Python knapsack DP. It should not be treated as settled until profiled — it could plausibly gain an order of magnitude.

5 · The compatibility score is unreconciled

Still marked [PROPOSED], and the paper it should reconcile with is not in the repo. It gates any drift-detection number.
